Frequently Asked Questions

Common Questions About ENT Care

What does an ENT doctor treat?

An ENT doctor diagnoses and treats conditions of the ear, nose and throat, including ear infections, hearing loss, sinus problems, nasal congestion, tonsil and adenoid issues, snoring, sleep apnea and breathing difficulties.

When should my child see an ENT?

Consider a visit if your child has repeated ear infections, frequent sore throats, loud snoring, constant mouth breathing, trouble hearing or delayed speech.

Can an ENT help with a speech delay?

Often, yes. Fluid in the ears, repeated infections and undiagnosed hearing loss can affect speech. A hearing and ear evaluation helps find out whether an ENT issue is involved.

Is snoring a sign of sleep apnea?

Not always, but loud, frequent snoring with pauses in breathing, daytime sleepiness or morning headaches should be evaluated. An ENT can look for airway causes and recommend next steps.

Does everyone with large tonsils need surgery?

No. Many patients are managed with monitoring or medical treatment. Surgery is considered when infections are frequent or when tonsils or adenoids affect breathing, sleep or hearing.

How do I know if I need a sinus specialist?

If congestion, facial pressure or sinus infections keep returning or do not improve with over-the-counter treatment, a specialist can find the cause and recommend lasting treatment.

What are common signs of hearing loss?

Asking people to repeat themselves, turning up the volume, trouble following conversations in noisy places and ringing in the ears are common signs.

What an Otolaryngologist Actually Does

Otolaryngology is the medical and surgical specialty devoted to the ears, nose, throat, and related structures of the head and neck. An otolaryngologist completes years of training beyond medical school, which includes both medical management and surgery. That is why one ENT can handle an ear infection in a toddler, a stubborn sinus problem in a parent, and a snoring issue in a grandparent.

The ears, nose, and throat are connected by shared passages. The Eustachian tube links the middle ear to the back of the nose. The sinuses drain into the nose. The throat sits at the crossroads of breathing, swallowing, and speaking. Because of these connections, a problem in one place often shows up somewhere else, and a specialist who understands the whole system can find the true source of the trouble.

When Should You See an ENT?

Consider booking a visit if you have congestion or facial pressure for more than ten days, repeated ear infections, a sore throat that keeps coming back, noticeable hearing changes, ringing in the ears, loud snoring, pauses in breathing at night, or a child who is slow to talk or to respond to sounds. If you have severe trouble breathing, call 911 or go to the nearest emergency room.

Understanding Ear Infections

Ear infections happen when fluid builds up behind the eardrum and becomes infected. Children are affected most often because their Eustachian tubes are shorter, more horizontal, and drain less easily than an adult’s. Common signs include ear pain, fever, fussiness, trouble sleeping, tugging at the ear, and muffled hearing. In adults, ear infections are less frequent but can be more serious, so persistent symptoms deserve attention.

Many ear infections improve on their own or with simple treatment, and your doctor will decide whether observation or medication is appropriate. The more important question is why the infections keep coming back. Allergies, enlarged adenoids, a recent cold, or exposure to smoke can all play a part. When infections are frequent, or fluid stays behind the eardrum for weeks and affects hearing, an ENT may discuss ear tubes, which are tiny tubes placed in the eardrum to let air in and fluid out. Whether they are right depends on your child’s age, hearing, and history.

Hearing Loss: Why Early Evaluation Matters

Hearing loss rarely announces itself. It tends to creep in so slowly that people adapt without noticing, until a family member mentions it. Common clues include asking people to repeat themselves, turning up the television, struggling in restaurants or crowded rooms, and hearing people speak but not understanding the words.

There are different types of hearing loss. Conductive loss comes from a problem in the outer or middle ear, such as earwax, fluid, or an infection, and is often treatable. Sensorineural loss involves the inner ear or the hearing nerve and is commonly related to aging, noise exposure, or other factors. Some people have a mix of both. A complete evaluation includes an examination of the ear and a hearing test, so that your doctor can tell which type you have and recommend the right path, whether that means treating the cause, monitoring, or hearing devices. Sudden hearing loss in one ear is different: it should be treated as urgent, so seek medical care promptly.

Tonsils and Adenoids in Children and Adults

Tonsils sit at the back of the throat and adenoids sit higher up behind the nose. Both are part of the body’s defense system, and both are largest in childhood. They are not a problem for most people. Trouble begins when they are repeatedly infected or so large that they get in the way of breathing, sleeping, or swallowing.

Signs that your child’s adenoids may be enlarged include a constantly stuffy nose, breathing through the mouth, snoring, a nasal-sounding voice, and repeated ear or sinus infections. Signs of troublesome tonsils include frequent sore throats, trouble swallowing, bad breath, and loud breathing at night. Decisions about surgery depend on how often the problems occur and how much they affect your child’s sleep, hearing, and daily life. Many children are simply monitored, because tonsils and adenoids often shrink as children grow.

Nasal Congestion and Sinus Problems

A stuffy nose has many causes. Allergies, colds, a deviated septum, enlarged turbinates, nasal polyps, and chronic sinus inflammation can all keep the nose from working properly. Persistent congestion can disturb your sleep, dull your sense of smell and taste, and lead to facial pressure and headaches. Treatment starts with finding the cause.

For many people, saline rinses, prescription nasal sprays, and allergy management bring real relief. If symptoms continue, your ENT may recommend a closer look with a small camera, imaging such as a CT scan, or a procedure to improve drainage. Using decongestant sprays for more than a few days is not advised, because they can cause rebound swelling and make congestion worse.

Snoring, Sleep Apnea and Breathing at Night

Snoring happens when relaxed tissues in the nose and throat vibrate as air moves past them. Occasional snoring is common. Loud, frequent snoring, especially with gasping, choking, or pauses in breathing, can be a sign of obstructive sleep apnea, a condition in which the airway repeatedly narrows or closes during sleep. Over time, untreated sleep apnea is linked to heavy daytime tiredness, morning headaches, trouble concentrating, and strain on the heart.

Children can have sleep-disordered breathing too, often from enlarged tonsils or adenoids. Instead of seeming sleepy, a child may appear restless, hyperactive, or have trouble at school. An ENT evaluates the airway to find where it narrows and may coordinate a sleep study to confirm the diagnosis. Options depend on the cause and the severity, and can include treating nasal blockage, addressing enlarged tissue, weight and lifestyle changes, a CPAP device, or surgery in selected cases.

Breathing Difficulties

Trouble breathing can have many origins, from the nose and throat down to the lungs and heart. An ENT focuses on the upper airway. A blocked nose, a deviated septum, enlarged tonsils, swelling in the throat, or a problem with the voice box can all make breathing feel hard or noisy. A careful examination helps identify whether the upper airway is involved and what can be done about it. Sudden or severe difficulty breathing is always an emergency, so call 911.

Speech Delay and the Hearing Connection

When a child is slow to talk, parents naturally want answers. Speech delay has many possible causes, and hearing is one of the first to check. Children learn language by listening, so even mild or temporary hearing loss from fluid in the ears can make it harder to pick up the sounds of speech. An ENT can examine the ears, arrange hearing testing, and look for related problems such as enlarged adenoids. Speech-language therapy is often the next step, and the two kinds of care work best together. Early checks give children the best chance to catch up.

ENT Care for Children: What Parents Should Know

Children are not small adults when it comes to ear, nose and throat health. Their ear tubes are shorter and more horizontal, which makes fluid harder to drain, and their tonsils and adenoids are naturally larger. This is why ear infections, snoring and mouth breathing are so common in early childhood, and why many of these problems improve as a child grows.

Still, some signs should not be ignored. A child who does not respond to quiet sounds, who watches your lips closely, who speaks less than other children of the same age or who has frequent ear or throat infections deserves a proper evaluation. Hearing is the foundation of speech, and even mild or temporary hearing loss can affect language development.

Adult ENT Concerns That Are Often Ignored

Adults often live with ENT symptoms for years because they have gotten used to them. Constant throat clearing, a stuffy nose, waking up with a dry mouth, ringing in the ears or turning the television up a little louder each year can all be signs of a treatable problem. Treating them can improve sleep, focus, energy and relationships. Hoarseness lasting more than a few weeks, trouble swallowing, hearing loss in one ear, or a lump in the neck should be checked without delay.

What Happens at Your First ENT Visit

A first visit typically begins with a conversation about your symptoms: when they started, how often they occur, what makes them better or worse, and what you have tried. We review your medical history, medications, and allergies. Then comes the examination, which may include looking in the ears, checking the nose and sinuses, and examining the mouth, throat, and neck. A thin, flexible scope with a tiny camera may be used to see deeper areas. Hearing tests or other studies may be recommended. Before you leave, we explain what we found and outline the options, so you know exactly what to expect next.

How to Prepare for Your Visit

A little preparation helps us find answers faster. Write down your symptoms, when they started and what makes them better or worse. Bring a list of current medications and any earlier test results, such as hearing tests or imaging. For a child, note how often ear infections or sore throats have happened, how your child sleeps and whether you have concerns about hearing or speech. Do not hesitate to ask anything you do not understand. You should leave with a clear diagnosis, treatment options and a plan for follow-up.

Everyday Habits That Support ENT Health

  • Keep headphone volume down and use ear protection around loud noise.
  • Avoid cotton swabs in the ear canal, which can push wax deeper.
  • Treat allergies early, before swelling blocks the nose and ears.
  • Rinse the nose with distilled, sterile, or boiled and cooled water only.
  • Stay hydrated and avoid smoke, which irritates the nose, throat and ears.
  • Wash hands regularly, and keep children’s routine checkups on schedule.

If symptoms continue despite these steps, a visit to your ENT is the best next move.

Questions to Ask Your ENT

Good care is a conversation. Feel free to ask what is causing your symptoms, which tests are needed and why, what the treatment options are, how long it may take to see improvement, and what the risks and alternatives are. If a procedure is suggested, ask what recovery involves and what happens if you choose to wait. A caring specialist will welcome these questions and will answer them in language you can follow, because informed patients make better decisions and tend to feel more confident about their care.

Know the Warning Signs

Some situations need urgent care. Call 911 or go to the nearest emergency room for severe trouble breathing, difficulty swallowing your own saliva, swelling of the face or throat, a nosebleed that will not stop, sudden hearing loss with dizziness, or a high fever with severe pain or swelling around the eyes or neck. These are not problems to wait on.
